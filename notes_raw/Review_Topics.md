# ME-3140 Machine Design: stress and section properties

This original review outline covers the stress concepts represented in the local machine-design lecture context and assignments. For an axially loaded prismatic member, average normal stress is force divided by cross-sectional area. A positive value denotes tension under the usual convention. In elementary elastic beam bending, the normal stress varies linearly with signed distance from the neutral axis; its magnitude is M c / I at the outer fiber. Here I is a second moment of area, with units of length to the fourth power. For a rectangle of width b and vertical height h, the horizontal centroidal second moment is b h cubed / 12. Keep the axis explicit: interchanging width and height changes the result. Bending formulas assume a suitable slender beam and linear elastic response.

For a solid circular shaft under elastic torsion, J = pi d to the fourth / 32, and maximum shear stress is 16 T / (pi d cubed). These expressions do not apply unchanged to noncircular sections. With torque fixed, increasing diameter by a factor r reduces peak torsional stress by r cubed. Convert N metres to N millimetres when section dimensions are in millimetres; one N per square millimetre is one MPa. A theoretical elastic stress concentration factor multiplies the nominal stress defined for its matching reference section.

For plane stress, the in-plane principal values are the average normal stress plus or minus the square root of the squared half difference plus squared in-plane shear. Include the zero out-of-plane principal stress when sorting all three principal values. Absolute maximum shear is half the difference between the largest and smallest of all three principal stresses. Principal planes carry normal traction and zero shear traction. Plane stress does not imply that the largest in-plane shear equals absolute maximum shear.

# ME-3010 System Dynamics: mechanical response and first-order circuits

This original review outline follows the mechanical modeling, Laplace-domain, and electrical-system topics represented in the local system-dynamics lectures. For translation, Newton's second law relates resultant force to mass times acceleration. For fixed-axis rigid-body rotation, resultant torque equals mass moment of inertia times angular acceleration. Mass moment of inertia has units kg metres squared, while the area moment used in beam bending has units metres to the fourth. State the axis and avoid confusing these quantities.

A linear spring opposes relative displacement from its unloaded state with force magnitude k times extension. A linear viscous damper opposes relative end velocity with force magnitude c times that velocity. They do not respond to the same state variable. Parallel springs share the same extension and their stiffnesses add. Series springs carry the same force and their deflections add, so stiffness reciprocals add. These equivalences assume massless ideal springs and the stated geometry.

A single-degree-of-freedom linear mass-spring-damper model is m x double dot plus c x dot plus k x equals the applied force. Its undamped angular natural frequency is the square root of k over m. Critical damping is twice the square root of k m. Damping ratio is c divided by critical damping. A ratio strictly between zero and one produces an underdamped free response. A ratio of one is critical damping. A ratio greater than one is overdamped. Oscillation period for the undamped model is two pi divided by angular natural frequency; angular frequency in radians per second differs from ordinary frequency in hertz.

For an ideal first-order series RC circuit, the time constant is R C. Convert resistance and capacitance to consistent SI units before multiplying. A stable real pole at minus a corresponds to an exponential decay with time constant one over a. The transfer-function output-to-input relation assumes zero initial conditions. Treat the response from nonzero initial conditions separately.
